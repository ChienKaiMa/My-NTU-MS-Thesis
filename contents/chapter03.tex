\chapter{Basics of Quantum State Discrimination}
\label{chap:basics_qsd}

This chapter introduces the fundamental concepts of quantum state
discrimination (QSD), providing the foundation for our contributions in
Chapters \ref{chap:error_uqsd} and \ref{chap:qc_qsd}.

In quantum communication, we adopt the standard scenario involving an
information sender (Alice) and a receiver (Bob).
Alice prepares a quantum state from a known set and transmits it to Bob, who
performs a measurement to identify the state \cite{qcqi}.
A key challenge arises with non-orthogonal states, which cannot be perfectly
distinguished due to the principles of quantum mechanics \cite{qcqi}.
This limitation underpins protocols like BB84 and motivates our study of
unambiguous quantum state discrimination (UQSD), minimum error discrimination (MED),
and our proposed CrossQSD and FitQSD problems.

\section{The Quantum State Discrimination Problem}
\label{sec:qsd_problem}
The QSD problem involves a set of \(k\) quantum states \(\{ \ket{\psi_i} \}_{i=1}^k\),
typically pure and non-orthogonal, shared between Alice and Bob, with
associated prior probabilities \(\{ p_i \}_{i=1}^k\), where \(p_i\) is the
likelihood that Alice prepares \(\ket{\psi_i}\).
Non-orthogonality is characterized by:
\begin{align}
    \langle \psi_i | \psi_j \rangle \neq 0, \quad \forall i \neq j, \quad 1 \leq i, j \leq k. \label{eq:non_orthogonal}
\end{align}
Bob’s task is to design a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
\(\{ \Pi_i \}_{i \in \mathcal{I}}\) to identify the transmitted state, subject to:
\begin{align}
    \Pi_i \succeq 0, \quad i \in \mathcal{I}, \quad \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} \Pi_i = I. \label{eq:povm_constraints}
\end{align}
Various figures of merit quantify discrimination performance \cite{RN65}.
For MED, the goal is to minimize the error probability, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:med}.
For UQSD, the objective is to achieve zero error probability with possible
inconclusive outcomes, as detailed in Section \ref{sec:uqsd}.
These approaches inform our development of CrossQSD and FitQSD in Chapter \ref{chap:error_uqsd}.

As an example, consider two non-orthogonal qubit states commonly used in QSD:
\begin{align}
    \ket{\psi_1} & = \ket{0}, \quad \ket{\psi_2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( \ket{0} + \ket{1} \right), \label{eq:qsd_example_states}
\end{align}
with equal prior probabilities \(p_1 = p_2 = 0.5\).
Their inner product, \(\langle \psi_1 | \psi_2 \rangle = 1/\sqrt{2} \neq 0\),
confirms non-orthogonality, making perfect discrimination impossible.
This elementary example, used in Subsection \ref{subsec:proj_meas}, Section
\ref{sec:med}, and Section \ref{sec:uqsd},
facilitates verification and understanding of QSD concepts, while more complex
challenges are addressed with the entangled and coherent states in
Subsections \ref{subsec:entangled_states} and \ref{subsec:coherent_states}.

\subsection{Projective Measurement Baseline}
\label{subsec:proj_meas}
A projective measurement is a POVM \(\{ \Pi_i \}_{i=1}^k\) where each operator
is Hermitian (\(\Pi_i^\dagger = \Pi_i\)), idempotent (\(\Pi_i^2 = \Pi_i\)),
and orthogonal (\(\Pi_i \Pi_j = \delta_{ij} \Pi_i\)), satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^k \Pi_i = I\).
For the example states in Equation \ref{eq:qsd_example_states}, consider a
standard projective measurement in the computational basis:
\begin{align}
    \Pi_1 & = \ket{0}\bra{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \Pi_2 = \ket{1}\bra{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \label{eq:proj_operators}
\end{align}
The success probability is:
\begin{align}
    P_{\text{succ}} & = p_1 \langle \psi_1 | \Pi_1 | \psi_1 \rangle + p_2 \langle \psi_2 | \Pi_2 | \psi_2 \rangle \notag \\
                    & = 0.5 \cdot 1 + 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.75, \label{eq:proj_psucc}
\end{align}
since \(\langle \psi_1 | \Pi_1 | \psi_1 \rangle = 1\), \(\langle \psi_1 | \Pi_2 | \psi_1 \rangle = 0\), \(\langle \psi_2 | \Pi_1 | \psi_2 \rangle = 0.5\), and \(\langle \psi_2 | \Pi_2 | \psi_2 \rangle = 0.5\).
Errors occur when Alice sends \(\ket{\psi_2}\) but Bob measures \(\Pi_1\) (50\% chance),
highlighting the limitations of projective measurements for non-orthogonal states.
This baseline result, compared to MED in Section \ref{sec:med}, motivates advanced QSD strategies.

\section{Minimum Error Discrimination}
\label{sec:med}
MED aims to distinguish a set of quantum states \(\{ \ket{\psi_i} \}_{i=1}^k\)
with prior probabilities \(\{ p_i \}_{i=1}^k\) by finding a POVM
\(\{ \Pi_i \}_{i=1}^k\) that minimizes the error probability, without allowing
inconclusive outcomes.
The error probability is:
\begin{align}
    P_{\text{err}} & = \sum_{i=1}^k \sum_{\substack{j=1                                                                           \\ j \neq i}}^k p_i \langle \psi_i | \Pi_j | \psi_i \rangle, \label{eq:med_error} \\
                   & = 1 - P_{\text{succ}} = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^k p_i \langle \psi_i | \Pi_i | \psi_i \rangle, \label{eq:med_success}
\end{align}
subject to:
\begin{align}
    \Pi_i \succeq 0, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k, \quad \sum_{i=1}^k \Pi_i = I. \label{eq:med_povm_constraints}
\end{align}
For \(k = 2\), the optimal POVM, known as the Helstrom measurement \cite{Barnett:09},
is analytically solvable to maximize:
\begin{align}
    P_{\text{succ}} & = p_1 \langle \psi_1 | \Pi_1 | \psi_1 \rangle + p_2 \langle \psi_2 | \Pi_2 | \psi_2 \rangle, \notag                                             \\
                    & = p_2 + \operatorname{Tr} \left[ \left( p_1 \ket{\psi_1}\bra{\psi_1} - p_2 \ket{\psi_2}\bra{\psi_2} \right) \Pi_1 \right], \label{eq:med_psucc}
\end{align}
where \(\Pi_2 = I - \Pi_1\).
For \(k > 2\), numerical methods like semidefinite programming are typically required.
The maximum \(P_{\text{succ}}\) occurs when \(\Pi_1\) projects onto the positive
eigenspace of \(p_1 \ket{\psi_1}\bra{\psi_1} - p_2 \ket{\psi_2}\bra{\psi_2}\).

For the example states in Equation \ref{eq:qsd_example_states}, with \(p_1 = p_2 = 0.5\):
\begin{align}
    p_1 \ket{\psi_1}\bra{\psi_1} - p_2 \ket{\psi_2}\bra{\psi_2} & = 0.5 \left( \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} \right) = 0.5 \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 \\ -0.5 & -0.5 \end{pmatrix}. \label{eq:med_operator}
\end{align}
The eigenvalues are \(\lambda_1 = \sqrt{2}/4\), \(\lambda_2 = -\sqrt{2}/4\), with eigenvectors:
\begin{align}
    v_1 & = \begin{pmatrix} -1 - \sqrt{2} & 1 \end{pmatrix}^T, \quad v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 + \sqrt{2} & 1 \end{pmatrix}^T. \notag
\end{align}
The POVM operator \(\Pi_1 = v_1 v_1^T / (v_1^T v_1)\) is:
\begin{align}
    \Pi_1 & = \frac{1}{4 + 2\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 3 + 2\sqrt{2} & -1 - \sqrt{2} \\ -1 - \sqrt{2} & 1 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.8536 & -0.3536 \\ -0.3536 & 0.1464 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \Pi_2 = I - \Pi_1. \label{eq:med_povm}
\end{align}
The success probability is:
\begin{align}
    P_{\text{succ}} & = 0.5 \langle \psi_1 | \Pi_1 | \psi_1 \rangle + 0.5 \langle \psi_2 | \Pi_2 | \psi_2 \rangle = \frac{2 + \sqrt{2}}{4} \approx 0.8536, \label{eq:med_psucc_result}
\end{align}
which matches the Helstrom bound \(P_{\text{succ}} = \frac{1 + \sqrt{1 - |\langle \psi_1 | \psi_2 \rangle|^2}}{2} = \frac{1 + 1/\sqrt{2}}{2}\).
This improvement over the projective measurement (\(P_{\text{succ}} = 0.75\))
demonstrates the advantage of optimized POVMs for non-orthogonal states.

\section{Unambiguous Quantum State Discrimination}
\label{sec:uqsd}
Unambiguous quantum state discrimination (UQSD) \cite{RN493, RN494, RN495}
aims to identify a quantum state from a set \(\{ \ket{\psi_i} \}_{i=1}^k\)
with zero error probability by using a POVM \(\{ \Pi_i \}_{i=0}^k\), where
\(\Pi_0\) corresponds to an inconclusive outcome.
Unlike minimum error discrimination (MED), UQSD allows inconclusive results to
ensure no misidentification.

For the example states in Equation \ref{eq:qsd_example_states},
\(\ket{\psi_1} = \ket{0}\), \(\ket{\psi_2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\ket{0} + \ket{1})\),
with prior probabilities \(p_1 = p_2 = 0.5\), an optimal UQSD POVM is:
\begin{align}
    \Pi_1 &= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{1 + \sqrt{2}} \frac{(\ket{0} - \ket{1})(\bra{0} - \bra{1})}{2} = \frac{1}{2 + \sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \label{eq:uqsd_pi1} \\
    \Pi_2 &= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{1 + \sqrt{2}} \ket{1}\bra{1} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{1 + \sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \label{eq:uqsd_pi2} \\
    \Pi_0 &= I - \Pi_1 - \Pi_2. \label{eq:uqsd_pi0}
\end{align}
The outcome probabilities, \( p(m|i) = \langle \psi_i | \Pi_m | \psi_i \rangle \), are:
\begin{align}
    p(1|1) &= 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.2929, \quad p(2|1) = 0, \quad p(0|1) \approx 0.7071, \notag \\
    p(1|2) &= 0, \quad p(2|2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}) \approx 0.2929, \quad p(0|2) \approx 0.7071. \notag
\end{align}
The average success probability is \( P_{\text{succ}} = \sum_{i=1}^2 p_i p(i|i) \approx 0.2929 \).
This POVM is optimal for \( k = 2 \), as it can be derived analytically \cite{RN493}.
For \( k > 2 \), numerical methods like semidefinite programming are required,
as discussed in Section \ref{sec:optimal_povm_for_uqsd}.

\section{The Holevo Bound}

The Holevo bound is a foundational result in quantum information theory that
establishes a fundamental limit on the amount of classical information that
can be extracted from an ensemble of quantum states.
It provides an upper bound on the classical mutual information between the
preparation of quantum states and the outcomes of a measurement performed on them.

Consider an ensemble of quantum states $\mathcal{E} = \{p_x, \rho_x\}$, where
the state $\rho_x$ is prepared with probability $p_x$.
Let the measurement be described by a set of positive-operator valued measure
(POVM) elements $\{M_y\}$, which results in a measurement outcome $y$.
The classical mutual information $I(X;Y)$ between the choice of state $X$ and
the measurement outcome $Y$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    I(X; Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X)
\end{equation}
where $H(Y)$ and $H(Y|X)$ are the classical Shannon entropy and conditional entropy, respectively.

The Holevo bound states that this classical mutual information is limited by
the Holevo information $\chi(\mathcal{E})$ of the ensemble.
The inequality is given by:
\begin{equation}
    I(X; Y) \leq S(\bar{\rho}) - \sum_x p_x S(\rho_x) = \chi(\mathcal{E})
\end{equation}
where $S(\cdot)$ is the von Neumann entropy and $\bar{\rho} = \sum_x p_x \rho_x$
is the average density matrix of the ensemble.

This inequality demonstrates that while an ensemble of non-orthogonal quantum
states may appear to carry a large amount of information, a single measurement
is fundamentally limited in its ability to extract all of it.
The Holevo bound reveals a crucial distinction between classical and quantum
information, highlighting the inaccessibility of information encoded in non-orthogonal states.
