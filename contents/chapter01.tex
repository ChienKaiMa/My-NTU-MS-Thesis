\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

Quantum state discrimination is a cornerstone of quantum information science,
enabling critical applications such as quantum cryptography, quantum
communication, and quantum computing \cite{bb84,RN492}.
The task of distinguishing non-orthogonal quantum states is particularly
challenging due to the fundamental limits imposed by quantum mechanics, which
prevent perfect discrimination of non-orthogonal states.
This challenge underpins protocols like BB84 \cite{bb84} and SARG04
\cite{RN492}, where non-orthogonal states, such as polarized single photons,
ensure secure communication by making eavesdropping detectable.

In practical quantum information protocols, quantum states are often prepared
in specific configurations, such as orthogonal states with basis changes or
symmetric states like trine states, to simplify measurement design.
However, in more general scenarios, discriminating arbitrary non-orthogonal
and linearly independent quantum states requires sophisticated measurement strategies.
Two prominent approaches are minimum error discrimination (MED), which
minimizes the probability of incorrect identification, and unambiguous quantum
state discrimination (UQSD), which prioritizes error-free identification at
the cost of inconclusive outcomes.
UQSD, for instance, plays a key role in protocols like B92 \cite{RN485}, where
it enables reliable state identification.

The presence of noise, whether in state preparation or measurement processes,
further complicates quantum state discrimination, necessitating robust
strategies to maintain performance in realistic settings.
These challenges motivate the development of efficient measurement designs and
their implementation on quantum hardware, particularly through quantum
circuits, to bridge theoretical protocols with practical quantum technologies.

\section{Our motivation}
Quantum state discrimination is pivotal for advancing quantum information
processing, enabling efficient quantum communication and computation in noisy environments.
A key challenge is implementing optimal discrimination strategies, such as
minimum error discrimination (MED) and unambiguous quantum state discrimination
(UQSD), on quantum hardware.
We were motivated to explore how these strategies can be realized as quantum
circuits, a critical step toward practical quantum technologies.

Our first question was how to construct quantum circuits for general quantum
state discrimination.
By leveraging Naimark's dilation theorem (see Section \ref{sec:naimark}), we
can transform positive operator-valued measures (POVMs) into projective
measurements or unitary operations, enabling circuit-based implementations.
Using state-of-the-art quantum circuit synthesis tools, we verified the
feasibility of these circuits for distinguishing non-orthogonal states.

We also investigated the impact of noise on discrimination performance.
Existing literature on mixed-state discrimination inspired us to explore
whether alternative measurement definitions could outperform standard USD
approaches, particularly for multi-qubit systems.
To isolate specific noise effects, we initially assume noiseless circuit
implementations while analyzing noisy input states, and vice versa.
These questions led us to define novel discrimination problems, addressed in
the following section.

\section{Our contributions}
In this thesis, we introduce two quantum state discrimination problems for noisy environments:
CrossQSD, which extends unambiguous state discrimination by incorporating
confidence bounds inspired by type-I and type-II errors in hypothesis testing,
and FitQSD, which optimizes POVMs to minimize changes to the output
probability distribution under noisy conditions.
We propose optimization strategies to design POVMs for these problems,
enhancing robustness in practical settings.
Additionally, we provide a concise proof that the probability of correctly
identifying the given state in the optimal MED scenario is always equal to or
larger than that in the Optimal UQSD scenario.

While quantum circuit synthesis for POVMs has been studied, a general synthesis
flow for arbitrary quantum state discrimination problems was previously lacking.
We develop a flow based on Naimark's theorem and isometry constructions
\cite{RN12}, enabling efficient circuit implementations.
For specific cases, such as coherent states relevant to optical quantum systems,
we demonstrate that encoding states with fewer qubits can significantly reduce
gate counts and circuit depth, albeit with a slight trade-off in discrimination
success probability.
These contributions pave the way for practical quantum state discrimination on
near-term quantum hardware.

\section{Thesis organization}
This thesis is structured as follows.
Chapter \ref{chap:basics_qc} introduces the fundamentals of quantum computation,
providing the necessary background for circuit-based implementations.
Chapter \ref{chap:basics_qsd} reviews quantum state discrimination, covering
key concepts like MED and UQSD.
Chapter \ref{chap:basics_co} outlines convex optimization techniques used in
our optimization strategies.
In Chapter \ref{chap:error_uqsd}, we develop noise-aware approaches for
quantum state discrimination, addressing challenges in CrossQSD and FitQSD.
Chapter \ref{chap:qc_qsd} presents our quantum circuit synthesis flow for
state discrimination and analyzes the impact of noise on circuits and input states.
Finally, Chapter \ref{chap:summary} summarizes our findings and discusses
future research directions to advance practical quantum state discrimination.
