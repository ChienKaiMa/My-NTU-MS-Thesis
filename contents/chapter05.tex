\chapter{Error-Tolerant Quantum State Discrimination}
\label{chap:error_uqsd}

% Revised by Google Gemini 2.5 Flash
In practical quantum systems, noise is an unavoidable factor that perturbs quantum states, causing them to deviate from ideal pure states.
This presence of noise is described by a mixed state, represented by a density matrix.
While Minimum Error Discrimination (MED) remains a viable strategy in the
presence of such noise, its performance can degrade.
Furthermore, the strict conditions required for Unambiguous State
Discrimination (UQSD) make it particularly susceptible to noise, rendering it
difficult or even impossible to achieve with certainty.

This chapter is dedicated to the problem of error-tolerant quantum state discrimination.
We will first establish a common theoretical framework for this scenario.
We will then review previous attempts to address this problem, and finally,
present our own perspectives and proposed solutions.
The chapter will conclude with a series of experiments and results from
different formulations, demonstrating their effectiveness under noisy conditions.

\section{Formal Setting for Noisy State Discrimination}
\label{sec:noisy_qsd_setting}
Assume we are given a predefined set of $k$ quantum states for discrimination,
represented by their respective density matrices $\{\rho_{1}, \rho_{2}, \dots, \rho_{k} \}$.
The prior probability for each input state $\rho_{i}$ is denoted by $p_{i}$,
where $i$ ranges from 1 to $k$. The sum of these prior probabilities is
normalized, such that $\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} = 1$.

The measurement is described by a POVM, defined by a set of measurement
operators $\{\Pi_{1}, \Pi_{2}, \dots, \Pi_{M}\}$.
The number of POVM elements, $M$, depends on the specific discrimination scheme.
For Minimum Error Discrimination, $M=k$, with each $\Pi_{i}$ corresponding to
the outcome that identifies state $\rho_{i}$.
For other schemes, such as Unambiguous State Discrimination or variants that
allow for inconclusive results, the measurement operators are typically
defined as $\{\Pi_{1}, \dots, \Pi_{k}, \Pi_{k + 1}\}$, where
$\Pi_{i}$ ($i=1,\dots,k$) corresponds to the outcome identifying state $\rho_{i}$,
and $\Pi_{k+1}$ is designed to represent an inconclusive result.

The discrimination process can be evaluated by calculating the joint
probabilities of a state being prepared and a specific outcome being obtained.
This can be organized into a "probability matrix" where each entry $p(m,i)$
represents the joint probability of obtaining outcome $m$ given that
state $\rho_{i}$ was prepared.
For a two-state scenario with a possible inconclusive outcome, the probability
matrix would be:

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c | c c c}
                   & $\Pi_{1}$ & $\Pi_{2}$ & $\Pi_{3}$ \\
        \hline
        $\rho_{1}$ & $p(1, 1)$ & $p(2, 1)$ & $p(3, 1)$ \\
        $\rho_{2}$ & $p(1, 2)$ & $p(2, 2)$ & $p(3, 2)$ \\
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Probability matrix for the discrimination of two states with a possible inconclusive outcome. The general entry is given by $p(m,i) = p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{m})$.}
    \label{tab:prob_mat}
\end{table}

From this probability matrix, we can derive several key performance metrics for
state discrimination.
The probability of successful discrimination is $P_{succ}$, the probability of
an erroneous outcome is $P_{err}$, and the probability of an inconclusive
outcome is $P_{inc}$.
These probabilities satisfy the following fundamental relations:
\begin{align}
     & P_{succ} + P_{err} + P_{inc} = 1 \\
     & \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} = 1
\end{align}

% Revised by Google Gemini 2.5 Flash
The utility of the density matrix formalism extends beyond merely representing
mixed states; it also offers a valuable framework for understanding how such
states can arise from pure states through noisy quantum processes.
In this context, we consider a scenario where the input states for
discrimination are subjected to a depolarizing channel with a noise factor $\lambda$,
transforming an initial pure state into a mixed state described by a density matrix.


\section{Modified Minimum Error Discrimination (MED+)}

We investigate a modified discrimination scheme, hereafter referred to as MED+.
This scheme extends standard Minimum Error Discrimination by explicitly
incorporating an operator for inconclusive outcomes, akin to Unambiguous
Quantum State Discrimination.
We are curious whether it would yield better $P_{succ}$ than MED.
In MED+, the measurement outcomes are categorized differently from a purely
error-minimizing approach. Specifically, we distinguish between:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conclusive Outcomes:} These are outcomes that uniquely identify a specific input state with a high probability.
          For example, if the measurement yields outcome $m$, it implies that state $\rho_m$ was sent, with minimal ambiguity.
    \item \textbf{Inconclusive Outcomes:} These are outcomes that do not provide sufficient information to reliably identify a specific input state.
          Such outcomes could potentially arise from any of the input states.
\end{itemize}

The semidefinite programming problem is formulated as follows:
\begin{align}
     & \underset{\{\Pi\}}{\text{maximize}} &  & P_{succ}=\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i}) \\
     & \text{subject to}                   &  & \Pi_{i} \succeq 0, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k+1                       \\
     &                                     &  & \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} \Pi_i = I
\end{align}


\begin{theorem} \label{thm:med_medplus}
    In the quantum state discrimination problem, given the same set of
    mixed quantum states $\{ \rho_{1}, \rho_{2}, \dots, \rho_{k} \}$
    and the same prior probabilities $\{p_{1}, p_{2}, \dots, p_{k}\}$, the\\
    probability of correctly identifying the given state in the optimal MED
    scenario is identical to that in the optimal MED+ scenario; i.e.,
    $(\psucc)\supmed = (\psucc)\supmedplus$.
    Furthermore, $\Pi_{k+1} = 0$ when the MED+ scenario becomes optimal.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    % Line break
    \noindent

    Let $\povm{k}$ be the collection of POVMs that each POVM has $k$ POVM
    elements, and here we allow a POVM element to be zero.
    Let $(\psucc)^{P}$ denote the probability of successful discrimination of
    some POVM $P$ or the POVM derived from some method $P$.
    We know that $(\psucc)\supmed >= (\psucc)^{P}, \; \forall \; P \in \povm{k}$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $(\psucc)\supmed \leq (\psucc)\supmedplus$\\
              Assume $\povm{MED}$ is an optimal solution for MED, and
              \begin{align*}
                  \povm{MED} \equiv \PiSet{k} \in \povm{k}.
              \end{align*}
              We can construct
              \begin{align*}
                  P \equiv \PiSetPrime{k+1} \in \povm{k+1}
              \end{align*}
              by
              \begin{align*}
                  \Pi_{1}' = \Pi_{1}, \; \Pi_{2}' = \Pi_{2}, \; \dots,
                  \; \Pi_{k}' = \Pi_{k}, \; \Pi_{k+1}' = 0
              \end{align*}
              Then,
              \begin{align}
                  (\psucc)\supmedplus & \geq (\psucc)^{P}
                  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i}')                      \\
                                      & = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i}) \\
                                      & = (\psucc)\supmed
              \end{align}
        \item $(\psucc)\supmed \geq (\psucc)\supmedplus$\\
              Assume $\povm{MED+}$ is an optimal solution for MED+, and
              \begin{align*}
                  \povm{MED+} \equiv \PiSet{k+1} \in \povm{k+1}.
              \end{align*}
              We can construct
              \begin{align*}
                  P \equiv \PiSetPrime{k} \in \povm{k}
              \end{align*}
              by
              \begin{align*}
                  \Pi_{1}' = \Pi_{1}, \; \Pi_{2}' = \Pi_{2}, \; \dots,
                  \; \Pi_{k}' = \Pi_{k} + \Pi_{k+1}
              \end{align*}
              Then,
              \begin{align}
                  (\psucc)\supmed & \geq (\psucc)^{P}
                  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i}')                           \\
                                  & = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i})
                  + p_{k} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{k+1})                                         \\
                                  & = (\psucc)\supmedplus + p_{k} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{k+1}) \\
                                  & \geq (\psucc)\supmedplus \label{eq:geq}
              \end{align}
        \item By 1. and 2., we can see that $(\psucc)\supmed = (\psucc)\supmedplus$.
        \item To make the equal sign in Eq (\ref{eq:geq}) hold, $\Pi_{k+1} = 0$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

From Theorem \ref{thm:med_medplus}, we can easily prove the
following corollary.
\begin{corollary}
    In the quantum state discrimination problem, given the same set of
    mixed quantum states $\{ \rho_{1}, \rho_{2}, \dots, \rho_{k} \}$
    and the same prior probabilities $\{p_{1}, p_{2}, \dots, p_{k}\}$, the \\
    probability of correctly identifying the given state in the optimal
    MED scenario is always equal to or larger than that in the Optimal UQSD
    scenario; i.e.,
    $(\psucc)\supmed \geq (\psucc)\supuqsd$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    If we see the MED+ problem and the Optimal UQSD problem as an convex
    optimization problem, then they have the same variables and the same
    objective function ($\psucc$), with the Optimal UQSD problem posing extra
    constraints on the error terms ($\perr = 0$).
    This means that the optimal solution of OptUQSD is always a feasible point
    of MED+, so $(\psucc)\supmedplus \geq (\psucc)\supuqsd$.
    Then $(\psucc)\supmed = (\psucc)\supmedplus \geq (\psucc)\supuqsd$.
\end{proof}

Even though the proof shows that MED+ won't provide any benefit over
MED, it helps as a bridge to build a more intuitive proof for the corollary.


\section{Fixed Rate of Inconclusive Outcome (FRIO)}
% Revised by Google Gemini 2.5 Flash
For the discrimination of mixed quantum states, a semidefinite programming
(SDP) approach was proposed by \cite{RN37}.
This formulation has been influential, serving as the theoretical basis for a
number of subsequent optical experiments \cite{RN48, RN221, RN29}.

A key feature of this formulation is the introduction of a constant real number,
$c_{lb}$, that directly controls the probability of an inconclusive outcome.
By imposing a constraint on the inconclusive POVM element $\Pi_{k+1}$, the
probability of an inconclusive outcome is lower bounded by some value $c_{lb}$.
In the numerical implementation using semidefinite programming, an inequality
constraint is employed to ensure that the rate of inconclusive outcomes is at least $c_{lb}$.
A value of $c_{lb} = 0$ corresponds to the minimum error discrimination problem,
where no inconclusive outcome is permitted.

This formulation, however, assumes a general mixed-state scenario.
If we assume that the mixed states arise from a well-defined channel acting on
pure states, it may be possible to reformulate the optimization problem to
exploit this structure, potentially accelerating the solving process.

As illustrated in Table \ref{tab:frio_prob_mat}, the joint probability for the
inconclusive outcome (indicated in yellow) is directly influenced by this
lower bound.

\begin{align}
     & \underset{\{\Pi\}}{\text{maximize}} &  & \raisebox{-0.3em}{\textcolor{green}{\rule{1em}{1em}}} \; P_{succ}=\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i})                 \\
     & \text{subject to}                   &  & \Pi_{i} \succeq 0, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k + 1                                                                                              \\

     &                                     &  & \raisebox{-0.3em}{\textcolor{yellow}{\rule{1em}{1em}}} \; P_{inc}=\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{k + 1}) \geq c_{lb}
\end{align}

One problem regarding to this formulation is that there is a

and we include a different set of constraints in the semidefinite programming
problem such that a desired alternative POVM can be obtained. Note that there
is an abundance of discussions about quantum hypothesis testing, which

% Assume a set of $k$ input quantum states are expressed in density matrices,

consider the error part of the conclusive results.
The formulation also resembles the maximum confidence discrimination scheme
\cite{RN519} and the scheme considering the worst-case a posteriori

The symbol $O_{i}$ denotes the event that the $i$-th outcome is measured.
The density matrices could be treated without assuming that the states are
