\chapter{Error-Tolerant Quantum State Discrimination}
\label{chap:error_uqsd}

% Revised by Google Gemini 2.5 Flash
In practical quantum systems, noise is an unavoidable factor that perturbs quantum states, causing them to deviate from ideal pure states.
This presence of noise is described by a mixed state, represented by a density matrix.
While Minimum Error Discrimination (MED) remains a viable strategy in the
presence of such noise, its performance can degrade.
Furthermore, the strict conditions required for Unambiguous State
Discrimination (UQSD) make it particularly susceptible to noise, rendering it
difficult or even impossible to achieve with certainty.

This chapter is dedicated to the problem of error-tolerant quantum state discrimination.
We will first establish a common theoretical framework for this scenario.
We will then review previous attempts to address this problem, and finally,
present our own perspectives and proposed solutions.
The chapter will conclude with a series of experiments and results from
different formulations, demonstrating their effectiveness under noisy conditions.

\section{Formal Setting for Noisy State Discrimination}
\label{sec:noisy_qsd_setting}
Assume we are given a predefined set of $k$ quantum states for discrimination,
represented by their respective density matrices $\{\rho_{1}, \rho_{2}, \dots, \rho_{k} \}$.
The prior probability for each input state $\rho_{i}$ is denoted by $p_{i}$,
where $i$ ranges from 1 to $k$. The sum of these prior probabilities is
normalized, such that $\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} = 1$.

The measurement is described by a POVM, defined by a set of measurement
operators $\{\Pi_{1}, \Pi_{2}, \dots, \Pi_{M}\}$.
The number of POVM elements, $M$, depends on the specific discrimination scheme.
For Minimum Error Discrimination, $M=k$, with each $\Pi_{i}$ corresponding to
the outcome that identifies state $\rho_{i}$.
For other schemes, such as Unambiguous State Discrimination or variants that
allow for inconclusive results, the measurement operators are typically
defined as $\{\Pi_{1}, \dots, \Pi_{k}, \Pi_{k + 1}\}$, where
$\Pi_{i}$ ($i=1,\dots,k$) corresponds to the outcome identifying state $\rho_{i}$,
and $\Pi_{k+1}$ is designed to represent an inconclusive result.

The discrimination process can be evaluated by calculating the joint
probabilities of a state being prepared and a specific outcome being obtained.
This can be organized into a "probability matrix" where each entry $p(m,i)$
represents the joint probability of obtaining outcome $m$ given that
state $\rho_{i}$ was prepared.
For a two-state scenario with a possible inconclusive outcome, the probability
matrix would be:

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c | c c c}
                   & $\Pi_{1}$ & $\Pi_{2}$ & $\Pi_{3}$ \\
        \hline
        $\rho_{1}$ & $p(1, 1)$ & $p(2, 1)$ & $p(3, 1)$ \\
        $\rho_{2}$ & $p(1, 2)$ & $p(2, 2)$ & $p(3, 2)$ \\
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Probability matrix for the discrimination of two states with a possible inconclusive outcome. The general entry is given by $p(m,i) = p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{m})$.}
    \label{tab:prob_mat}
\end{table}

From this probability matrix, we can derive several key performance metrics for
state discrimination.
The probability of successful discrimination is $\psucc$, the probability of
an erroneous outcome is $\perr$, and the probability of an inconclusive
outcome is $\pinc$.
These probabilities satisfy the following fundamental relations:
\begin{align}
     & \psucc + \perr + \pinc = 1 \\
     & \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} = 1
\end{align}

% Revised by Google Gemini 2.5 Flash
The utility of the density matrix formalism extends beyond merely representing
mixed states; it also offers a valuable framework for understanding how such
states can arise from pure states through noisy quantum processes.
In this context, we consider a scenario where the input states for
discrimination are subjected to a depolarizing channel with a noise factor $\lambda$,
transforming an initial pure state into a mixed state described by a density matrix.


\section{Modified Minimum Error Discrimination (MED+)}

We investigate a modified discrimination scheme, hereafter referred to as MED+.
This scheme extends standard Minimum Error Discrimination by explicitly
incorporating an operator for inconclusive outcomes, akin to Unambiguous
Quantum State Discrimination.
We are curious whether it would yield better $\psucc$ than MED.
In MED+, the measurement outcomes are categorized differently from a purely
error-minimizing approach. Specifically, we distinguish between:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conclusive Outcomes:} These are outcomes that uniquely identify a specific input state with a high probability.
          For example, if the measurement yields outcome $m$, it implies that state $\rho_m$ was sent, with minimal ambiguity.
    \item \textbf{Inconclusive Outcomes:} These are outcomes that do not provide sufficient information to reliably identify a specific input state.
          Such outcomes could potentially arise from any of the input states.
\end{itemize}

The semidefinite programming problem is formulated as follows:
\begin{align}
     & \underset{\{\Pi\}}{\text{maximize}} &  & \psucc=\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i}) \\
     & \text{subject to}                   &  & \Pi_{i} \succeq 0, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k+1                     \\
     &                                     &  & \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} \Pi_i = I
\end{align}


\begin{theorem} \label{thm:med_medplus}
    In the quantum state discrimination problem, given the same set of
    mixed quantum states $\{ \rho_{1}, \rho_{2}, \dots, \rho_{k} \}$
    and the same prior probabilities $\{p_{1}, p_{2}, \dots, p_{k}\}$, the\\
    probability of correctly identifying the given state in the optimal MED
    scenario is identical to that in the optimal MED+ scenario; i.e.,
    $(\psucc)\supmed = (\psucc)\supmedplus$.
    Furthermore, $\Pi_{k+1} = 0$ when the MED+ scenario becomes optimal.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    % Line break
    \noindent

    Let $\povm{k}$ be the collection of POVMs that each POVM has $k$ POVM
    elements, and here we allow a POVM element to be zero.
    Let $(\psucc)^{P}$ denote the probability of successful discrimination of
    some POVM $P$ or the POVM derived from some method $P$.
    We know that $(\psucc)\supmed >= (\psucc)^{P}, \; \forall \; P \in \povm{k}$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $(\psucc)\supmed \leq (\psucc)\supmedplus$\\
              Assume $\povm{MED}$ is an optimal solution for MED, and
              \begin{align*}
                  \povm{MED} \equiv \PiSet{k} \in \povm{k}.
              \end{align*}
              We can construct
              \begin{align*}
                  P \equiv \PiSetPrime{k+1} \in \povm{k+1}
              \end{align*}
              by
              \begin{align*}
                  \Pi_{1}' = \Pi_{1}, \; \Pi_{2}' = \Pi_{2}, \; \dots,
                  \; \Pi_{k}' = \Pi_{k}, \; \Pi_{k+1}' = 0
              \end{align*}
              Then,
              \begin{align}
                  (\psucc)\supmedplus & \geq (\psucc)^{P}
                  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i}')                      \\
                                      & = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i}) \\
                                      & = (\psucc)\supmed
              \end{align}
        \item $(\psucc)\supmed \geq (\psucc)\supmedplus$\\
              Assume $\povm{MED+}$ is an optimal solution for MED+, and
              \begin{align*}
                  \povm{MED+} \equiv \PiSet{k+1} \in \povm{k+1}.
              \end{align*}
              We can construct
              \begin{align*}
                  P \equiv \PiSetPrime{k} \in \povm{k}
              \end{align*}
              by
              \begin{align*}
                  \Pi_{1}' = \Pi_{1}, \; \Pi_{2}' = \Pi_{2}, \; \dots,
                  \; \Pi_{k}' = \Pi_{k} + \Pi_{k+1}
              \end{align*}
              Then,
              \begin{align}
                  (\psucc)\supmed & \geq (\psucc)^{P}
                  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i}')                           \\
                                  & = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i})
                  + p_{k} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{k+1})                                         \\
                                  & = (\psucc)\supmedplus + p_{k} \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{k+1}) \\
                                  & \geq (\psucc)\supmedplus \label{eq:geq}
              \end{align}
        \item By 1. and 2., we can see that $(\psucc)\supmed = (\psucc)\supmedplus$.
        \item To make the equal sign in Eq (\ref{eq:geq}) hold, $\Pi_{k+1} = 0$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

From Theorem \ref{thm:med_medplus}, we can easily prove the
following corollary.
\begin{corollary}
    In the quantum state discrimination problem, given the same set of
    mixed quantum states $\{ \rho_{1}, \rho_{2}, \dots, \rho_{k} \}$
    and the same prior probabilities $\{p_{1}, p_{2}, \dots, p_{k}\}$, the \\
    probability of correctly identifying the given state in the optimal
    MED scenario is always equal to or larger than that in the Optimal UQSD
    scenario; i.e.,
    $(\psucc)\supmed \geq (\psucc)\supuqsd$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    If we see the MED+ problem and the Optimal UQSD problem as an convex
    optimization problem, then they have the same variables and the same
    objective function ($\psucc$), with the Optimal UQSD problem posing extra
    constraints on the error terms ($\perr = 0$).
    This means that the optimal solution of OptUQSD is always a feasible point
    of MED+, so $(\psucc)\supmedplus \geq (\psucc)\supuqsd$.
    Then $(\psucc)\supmed = (\psucc)\supmedplus \geq (\psucc)\supuqsd$.
\end{proof}

Even though the proof shows that MED+ won't provide any benefit over
MED, it helps as a bridge to build a more intuitive proof for the corollary.


\section{Fixed Rate of Inconclusive Outcome (FRIO)}
% Revised by Google Gemini 2.5 Flash
For the discrimination of mixed quantum states, a semidefinite programming
(SDP) approach was proposed by \cite{RN37}.
This formulation has been influential, serving as the theoretical basis for a
number of subsequent optical experiments \cite{RN48, RN221, RN29}.

A key feature of this formulation is the introduction of a constant real number,
$c_{lb}$, that directly controls the probability of an inconclusive outcome.
By imposing a constraint on the inconclusive POVM element $\Pi_{k+1}$, the
probability of an inconclusive outcome is lower bounded by some value $c_{lb}$.
In the numerical implementation using semidefinite programming, an inequality
constraint is employed to ensure that the rate of inconclusive outcomes is at least $c_{lb}$.
A value of $c_{lb} = 0$ corresponds to the minimum error discrimination problem,
where no inconclusive outcome is permitted.

This formulation, however, assumes a general mixed-state scenario.
If we assume that the mixed states arise from a well-defined channel acting on
pure states, it may be possible to reformulate the optimization problem to
exploit this structure, potentially accelerating the solving process.

As illustrated in Table \ref{tab:frio_prob_mat}, the joint probability for the
inconclusive outcome (indicated in yellow) is directly influenced by this
lower bound.

The semidefinite programming problem can be formulated as follows:
\begin{align}

     & \text{subject to}                   &  & \Pi_{i} \succeq 0, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k + 1                                                                                            \\
     &                                     &  & \sum_{i=1}^{k + 1} \Pi_i = I                                                                                                             \\
     &                                     &  & \raisebox{-0.3em}{\textcolor{yellow}{\rule{1em}{1em}}} \; \pinc=\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{k + 1}) \geq c_{lb}
\end{align}


\section{CrossQSD}
\label{sec:crossqsd}
Inspired by type-I and type-II errors in hypothesis testing, CrossQSD
formulates a semidefinite programming (SDP) problem with novel constraints to
design a POVM for quantum state discrimination (QSD) that is robust to noise
and retains unambiguous QSD (UQSD) characteristics.
Unlike FRIO, which assumes fixed mixed states \(\{ \rho_i \}_{i=1}^k\),
CrossQSD targets pure states \(\{ \ket{\psi_i} \}_{i=1}^k\) degraded into
mixed states by noise, aiming for a POVM effective under specific noise yet
functional with reduced or no noise.
By treating \(\rho_i\) as pure states and setting \(\alpha_i = \beta_i = 0\),
CrossQSD degenerates to UQSD, which often fails in noisy environments.
Our SDP achieves robustness by optimizing over flexible error constraints,
resembling maximum confidence discrimination \cite{RN519} and worst-case a
posteriori probability schemes \cite{kosut2004quantumstatedetectordesign}.

The symbol \(O_i\) denotes the event of measuring the \(i\)-th outcome.
The SDP uses density matrices \(\{ \rho_i \}_{i=1}^k\), assumed to be noisy
versions of pure states.
The SDP formulation is as follows:
\begin{align}
    \underset{\{\Pi_i\}}{\text{maximize}} \quad & \psucc = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_i \Pi_i) \label{eq:crossqsd_obj}                                                                                                   \\
    \text{subject to} \quad                     & \Pi_i \succeq 0, \quad i = 0, \ldots, k \label{eq:crossqsd_psd}                                                                                                                       \\
                                                & \sum_{i=0}^{k} \Pi_i = I \label{eq:crossqsd_sum}                                                                                                                                      \\
                                                & P(O_i \mid \rho_i) = \frac{p_i \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_i \Pi_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^k p_j \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_j \Pi_i)} \geq 1 - \alpha_i, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k \label{eq:crossqsd_type1} \\
                                                & P(\rho_i \mid O_i) = \frac{p_i \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_i \Pi_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^k p_j \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_j \Pi_i)} \geq 1 - \beta_i, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k \label{eq:crossqsd_type2}
\end{align}

To illustrate CrossQSD’s performance, we consider three coherent states truncated to 3 qubits, with eigenvalues defined by amplitudes \([1, 1, 1]\) and angles \([0, \pi/3, 2\pi/3]\). OptUQSD, applied to these pure states, discards the second state, yielding \(\psucc = 0.2343\). For CrossQSD, we solve the SDP with input density matrices \(\rho_i = (1-\lSDP)\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i} + \lSDP I/d\), using a fixed depolarizing noise parameter \(\lSDP = 0.01\) and error tolerances \(\alpha_i = \beta_i = 0.01\), where \(d\) is the dimension of the Hilbert space. The resulting POVM is evaluated with density matrices \(\rho_i = (1-\leval)\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i} + \leval I/d\), where \(\leval\) varies, computing \(\psucc\), \(\perr\), and their ratio \(\perr / \psucc\), as shown in Figure \ref{fig:crossqsd_results} and Table \ref{tab:crossqsd_results}. The resulting POVM depend on \(\lSDP\), \(\alpha_i\), and \(\beta_i\); setting \(\lSDP \approx \alpha_i, \beta_i\) ensures non-trivial solutions, as large \(\lSDP\) leads to an inconclusive POVM (\(\Pi_0 = I\)), while small \(\lSDP\) limits robustness to a narrow noise range.

\section{FitQSD}
Since we know the optimal POVM for unambiguously
discriminating pure states through noiseless channels,
we ask if we can find a POVM such that the resulting probability distribution
remains unchanged or changes the least.
There are two things that need to be well-defined: "probability distribution"
and "distance between two probability distributions".
Different definitions will lead to very different solutions and results.

The first definition we came up with, for the target probability distribution,
is the probability distribution of the measurement outcomes of the POVM.
The probability distribution $\{\pi\} \equiv \{\pi_{0}, \pi_{1}, \dots, \pi_{k}\}$,
where $\pi_{i} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} p_{j} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{j} \Pi_{i})$.
This definition sums the "error" terms with the "correct" terms as long as
they belong to the same measurement operator. While this works when the errors
are relatively small, we hoped to define a more meaningful problem.

The modified definition of "probability distribution" separates the "correct"
terms and "error" terms.
Another nontrivial part is whether we separate the inconclusive part.
A relatively trivial definition uses the probability matrix discussed in
previous sections. We evaluate the distance by first flattening the matrices,
then calculating the distances between two vectors.

Assume the number of states is $k$, define the probability matrix $\textnormal{ProbMat}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times (k+1)}$.

We can investigate different formulations with the same definition of the
probability distribution and distance between two distributions.
We have tried to make the names for these formulations straight forward.



\subsection{Minimizing \texorpdfstring{$L_1$}{L1} Distance to Approximate UQSD}
For a matrix $A$ with entries $a_{m,n}$, the "entry-wise $L_p$ norm" for
$p \geq 1$ is defined as:
\begin{align}
    \left\| A \right\|_{p} \triangleq \left( \sum_{m=1}^{M} \sum_{n=1}^{N} |a_{m, n}| ^{p} \right) ^ {\frac{1}{p}}.
\end{align}
This definition is equivalent to the vector $p$-norm of the vectorized form of
the matrix, as expressed by $\left\| A \right\|_{p} = \left\| \text{vec}(A) \right\|_{p}$.

In this work, we use the $L_1$ norm to quantify the difference between two
probability matrices.
Let $Q$ be the ideal probability matrix for Unambiguous Quantum State
Discrimination (UQSD) and $P$ be the probability matrix of a POVM that we are trying to optimize.
We define the $L_1$ distance between these two matrices as $\left\| P-Q \right\|_{1}$.

Our objective is to find a POVM that minimizes this $L_1$ distance.
By minimizing $\left\| P-Q \right\|_{1}$, we obtain a measurement scheme whose
performance, when applied to the given noisy quantum states, approximates the
ideal UQSD outcome as closely as possible in an $L_1$ sense.

The semidefinite programming problem can be formulated as follows:
\begin{align}
     & \underset{\{\Pi\}}{\text{minimize}} &  & ||P-Q||_{1}                               \\
     & \text{subject to}                   &  & \Pi_{i} \succeq 0, \quad i = 0, \ldots, k \\
     &                                     &  & \sum_{i=0}^{k} \Pi_i = I
\end{align}

\subsection{Minimizing the Sum of Squares to Approximate UQSD}
An alternative to minimizing the $L_1$ distance is to minimize the sum of
squares of the differences between the probability matrices.
This approach, equivalent to minimizing the squared $L_2$ norm, is often
preferred in optimization due to its mathematical properties, such as being a
continuously differentiable function.
The squared $L_2$ norm penalizes large errors more heavily than the $L_1$ norm,
which can be desirable for finding a more balanced solution.

Let $Q$ be the ideal UQSD probability matrix and $P$ be the probability matrix
of our desired POVM, where the entries are $q_{m,i}$ and $p_{m,i}$ respectively. The sum of squares, denoted as $S$, is defined as:
\begin{align}
    S \triangleq \sum_{m,i} \left(p_{m,i} - q_{m,i}\right)^2.
\end{align}
This objective function, $S$, is a convex quadratic function of the POVM elements,
which allows the optimization problem to be formulated as a semidefinite program (SDP).
By minimizing $S$, we seek to find a POVM that minimizes the total squared
deviation from the ideal UQSD probability matrix.
This provides a different criterion for what constitutes an "optimal"
approximation, emphasizing the reduction of larger discrepancies.
However, in our experiments, this approach did not yield better results
compared to minimizing the $L_1$ distance, due to the precision limitations of
the numerical solvers used, particularly when dealing with the specific problem
instances and their inherent numerical challenges.

\begin{align}
     & \underset{\{\Pi\}}{\text{minimize}} &  & S                                         \\
     & \text{subject to}                   &  & \Pi_{i} \succeq 0, \quad i = 0, \ldots, k \\
     &                                     &  & \sum_{i=0}^{k} \Pi_i = I
\end{align}

\subsection{Minimizing Error with Constrained Overlap}
\label{sec:maxpsucc_mindiff}

A novel approach to approximate Unambiguous Quantum State Discrimination (UQSD)
is to modify the standard Minimum Error Discrimination (MED) problem.
This method, which we term the "Minimizing Error with Constrained Overlap (MECO)"
scheme, maximizes the probability of success while imposing constraints on the
joint probabilities of the measurement outcomes.
The objective is to push the solution toward a form where there is minimal
overlap between different states' outcome probabilities, a key characteristic of UQSD.

The semidefinite programming (SDP) problem is formulated as follows:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:maxpsucc_mindiff_obj}
     & \underset{\{\Pi\}}{\text{maximize}} &  & \psucc = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{i})                                   \\
    \label{eq:maxpsucc_mindiff_constr1}
     & \text{subject to}                   &  & \Pi_{i} \succeq 0, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k+1                                                         \\
    \label{eq:maxpsucc_mindiff_constr2}
     &                                     &  & \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} \Pi_i = I                                                                          \\
    \label{eq:maxpsucc_mindiff_constr3}
     &                                     &  & p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{j}) \leq p(i, j), \quad \text{for } i = j, \; i=1,\ldots,k    \\
    \label{eq:maxpsucc_mindiff_constr4}
     &                                     &  & p_{i} \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{i} \Pi_{j}) \geq p(i, j), \quad \text{for } i \neq j, \; i=1,\ldots,k
\end{align}
In this formulation, the objective function is to maximize the overall success probability, $\psucc$.
The constraints in Equations (\ref{eq:maxpsucc_mindiff_constr1}) and (\ref{eq:maxpsucc_mindiff_constr2}) define a valid POVM.
The new constraints in (\ref{eq:maxpsucc_mindiff_constr3}) and (\ref{eq:maxpsucc_mindiff_constr4})
are designed to control the entries of the probability matrix.
Specifically, these constraints impose a lower bound on the off-diagonal
entries and an upper bound on the diagonal entries.
This effectively forces the solution to resemble a UQSD matrix, where the
off-diagonal terms are zero and the diagonal terms are maximized, but within
the confines of a minimum error formulation.
A crucial observation is that changing the signs of these inequalities would
lead to a problem similar to standard Minimum Error Discrimination, which does
not enforce this behavior.

\section{Trade-off between MED and UQSD}
To create a hybrid discrimination scheme that balances a high success
probability with an outcome distribution that approximates Unambiguous Quantum
State Discrimination (UQSD), we formulate the following problem.
This approach seeks to find a POVM that maximizes the probability of
successful discrimination ($\psucc$) while simultaneously minimizing its
$L_1$ distance from an ideal UQSD probability matrix.
This trade-off is managed by introducing a weighting coefficient, $w$.

The semidefinite programming (SDP) problem is formulated as follows:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:roland_obj}
     & \underset{\{\Pi\}}{\text{maximize}} &  & \psucc - w \|P-Q\|_{1}                      \\
     & \text{subject to}                   &  & \Pi_{i} \succeq 0, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k+1 \\
     &                                     &  & \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} \Pi_i = I
\end{align}
Here, $P$ is the probability matrix for the POVM defined by the set of
operators $\{\Pi_i\}$, and $Q$ is the target UQSD probability matrix.
The objective function in Equation \ref{eq:roland_obj} balances the
maximization of $\psucc$ with the minimization of the $L_1$ distance between $P$ and $Q$.
The constraints ensure that the set of operators $\{\Pi_i\}$ constitutes a valid POVM.
The weighting coefficient $w \geq 0$ allows us to tune the importance of
minimizing the $L_1$ distance relative to maximizing the success probability.

To evaluate the trade-off between MED and UQSD, we apply the hybrid
discrimination scheme to two state sets: two non-orthogonal states
(Chapter 3, Equation \ref{eq:qsd_example_states}) and two-qubit entangled
states with parameters $a_1 = 0.2$, $a_2 = 0.5$, $a_3 = 0.7$
(Chapter 2, Subsection \ref{subsec:entangled_states}, Equation \ref{eq:entangled_state_set}),
both with equal prior probabilities.
Input states are pure states mixed with depolarizing noise parameterized by
$\lSDP$, and $\psucc$ is evaluated on these noisy states.
Results are compared against MED and OptUQSD baselines for pure states,
plotted as horizontal lines in the figures (Figures \ref{fig:entangled_psucc},
\ref{fig:entangled_l2}, \ref{fig:nonortho_psucc}, \ref{fig:nonortho_l2}).
The SDP solution, tuned by the weighting coefficient $w$, shows a positive
correlation between $\psucc$ and $L_2$ distance, saturating to MED or
UQSD at extreme $w$ values, causing overlapping lines in the figures;
determining the effective $w$ range requires trial and error, a notable drawback.
At high $\lSDP$, results become less interpretable due to
significant noise effects, consistent with expected degradation in discrimination performance.
